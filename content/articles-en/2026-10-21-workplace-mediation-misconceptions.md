When conflict takes hold within a team, two reflexes tend to dominate. Either we wait for it to resolve itself, or we immediately hand it over to a formal process. Between these two extremes, mediation remains an underused option in our organizations. Yet when it is properly structured, it allows the people involved to regain control of the situation and build a solution of their own that stands the test of time.

If managers and employees still hesitate, it is often because of persistent misconceptions. Here are five that I regularly encounter in the field, and what the reality actually is.

## 1. “Mediation is for minor disagreements”

Mediation is sometimes associated with trivial misunderstandings. In practice, it often proves most useful once a conflict has escalated, whether it involves a broken relationship between colleagues, ongoing tension between a manager and her team, or a work climate that has deteriorated to the point of affecting attendance. The older and more emotionally charged a conflict is, the more a neutral third party can help the parties move beyond entrenched positions and return to what truly matters to each of them.

What determines whether mediation is appropriate, then, is not the scale of the conflict. It is rather the minimum willingness of those involved to look for a way forward and their ability to engage in discussion on an equal footing.

## 2. “The mediator will decide who is right”

A mediator is neither a judge, an arbitrator, nor an investigator. They make no decision and do not rule on the merits of anyone’s position. Their role is to structure the discussion, ensure that each person can speak and be heard, and help the parties clarify their needs and interests.

The solution, when one emerges, belongs to the parties. That is precisely what makes it more lasting than a decision imposed from outside. This approach requires genuine impartiality, with no connection to either party and no stake in the outcome. It is one of the concrete advantages of calling on an external practitioner rather than someone within the organization.

## 3. “If mediation fails, I will have lost my recourse”

This fear discourages many people from trying. Yet mediation is voluntary from start to finish. Each participant may withdraw if they feel it no longer suits them, and the absence of an agreement does not close the door to a grievance, a complaint, or proceedings before a tribunal. In addition, discussions held in mediation are confidential, and the agreement signed at the outset says so explicitly. What is said there is not meant to be used elsewhere.

One precaution is in order, however. Time limits for filing a claim generally continue to run during the process. For example, a psychological harassment complaint under Quebec’s Act respecting labour standards must be filed within two years of the last incidence of the conduct. In unionized workplaces, the grievance time limits set out in the collective agreement apply. It is therefore wise to check these deadlines before beginning the process.

## 4. “Agreeing to mediation means admitting fault”

Taking part in mediation is neither an admission nor a commitment to make concessions. It is a space to understand the other person’s perspective, name one’s own needs, and explore solutions that no one would have thought of alone. The process looks toward the future of the working relationship rather than toward finding someone to blame.

For a manager, proposing mediation is not a sign of weakness either. On the contrary, it is a way to act early, to show that the organization is taking the situation seriously, and to preserve working relationships that still have value.

## 5. “Mediation can replace a harassment investigation”

This is likely the misconception with the most serious consequences. Quebec’s Act respecting labour standards requires employers to take reasonable action to prevent psychological harassment and, whenever they become aware of such behaviour, to put a stop to it. When allegations of harassment are made, particularly of a sexual nature, or when there is a marked power imbalance or a climate of fear, mediation is generally not the appropriate first step. A rigorous assessment of the situation and, where necessary, an investigation conducted by a competent and impartial person are then required.

This does not mean mediation is ruled out. It can have its place once the facts have been established, to help people rebuild a functional working relationship and support the return to a healthy work environment. The key challenge is to distinguish interpersonal conflict from harassment and to choose the right process at the right time.

## How to know whether mediation is right for your situation

Before recommending mediation, it is essential to assess the nature of the dispute, the balance of power between the people involved, their readiness to engage, and the applicable legal framework, particularly in unionized settings. This preliminary assessment avoids proposing an unsuitable process and protects both the individuals and the organization.
