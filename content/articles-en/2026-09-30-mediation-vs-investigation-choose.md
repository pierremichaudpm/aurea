## Direct answer

Mediation is appropriate when both parties acknowledge that a dispute exists, remain able to communicate, and wish to preserve their working relationship. An investigation is required when there are specific allegations of wrongdoing, such as harassment, misconduct, or abuse of authority, when there is a power imbalance between the parties, or when one party refuses to participate in a voluntary process. In short, mediation repairs a relationship, whereas an investigation establishes the facts and determines whether there has been a breach of a legal obligation or internal policy.

This choice is not always mutually exclusive. In some cases, an investigation should precede mediation, or mediation may follow an investigation to restore the working relationship once the facts have been established.

## Key differences between mediation and investigation

Mediation aims to restore communication and help the parties reach an agreement, whereas an investigation seeks to establish the facts and determine whether an alleged breach occurred. Mediation is voluntary, and the parties retain control over the outcome; an investigation, by contrast, is directed by the employer, and its outcome is determined by the investigator. From a legal standpoint, mediation is a consensual and often confidential process, while an investigation is governed by the employer’s obligations regarding psychological health and harassment. Mediation results in an agreement negotiated between the parties, whereas an investigation produces a report containing findings and, in some cases, disciplinary recommendations. The mediator acts as an impartial facilitator without decision-making authority, while the investigator is independent and held to evidentiary rigor.

## Decision-making criteria

Several factors influence the choice between mediation and investigation. No single criterion is determinative on its own, but their combination usually points fairly quickly to the most appropriate approach.

• Nature of the allegations: Relationship conflicts or personality clashes generally point toward mediation. Allegations of harassment, discrimination, or abuse of authority require an investigation.
• Balance of power: Where there is a significant imbalance, for example between a manager and an employee reporting directly to them, mediation may reproduce the imbalance rather than resolve it.
• Parties’ willingness: Mediation requires the free and informed participation of both parties. If one party refuses to participate or fears retaliation, an investigation becomes the appropriate avenue.
• Employer legal obligations: When a complaint involves obligations under labour or occupational health and safety legislation, the employer must document a rigorous process, which only an investigation can provide.
• Severity and repetition of the alleged conduct: An isolated and less serious incident may be suitable for mediation. Repeated conduct or a serious attack on an individual's dignity calls for an investigation.
• Organizational objective: Restoring a viable working relationship points toward mediation. Determining responsibility and making informed disciplinary decisions requires an investigation.

## When both approaches are combined

In many situations, mediation and investigation are not mutually exclusive. Two common scenarios arise.

Investigation followed by mediation. Once the facts have been established and appropriate measures determined, mediation can help the parties rebuild a functional working relationship, particularly when they must continue working together. This stage is distinct from the investigation and should never be used to dilute its conclusions.

Mediation suspended in favour of an investigation. Sometimes a mediation process reveals more serious allegations than initially presented, or a party no longer feels able to continue the discussion safely. In such cases, the mediator should end the process and recommend a formal investigation without attempting to determine the facts personally.

In both scenarios, the individual responsible for the file must remain attentive to indicators that a change in approach is required rather than adhering to the original mandate out of habit.

## Frequently asked questions

Can mediation be imposed on an unwilling party? No. Mediation is based on the voluntary participation of both parties. If one refuses to participate, the matter should be directed to an investigation or another appropriate process.

Can mediation replace an investigation when harassment allegations are made? No, not when the allegations are specific and potentially substantiated. The employer has a duty to determine the relevant facts, which mediation, focused on dialogue rather than fact-finding, cannot accomplish.

Who can act as a mediator or investigator in the workplace? In both cases, it is recommended to retain an independent professional with appropriate training and no reporting relationship to the parties involved, often a CRIA or CRHA accredited as a mediator. A person who acted as investigator in a matter should not subsequently serve as mediator in the same matter because they have already formed conclusions regarding the facts, compromising the neutrality required for mediation.

How long does each process take? Mediation is generally completed in one or two sessions over a period of several weeks. An investigation takes longer because it involves gathering witness evidence, reviewing information, and preparing a report, typically over several weeks depending on the complexity of the matter.
