As soon as a psychological harassment complaint is received, the employer has a legal obligation to act diligently. The employer must acknowledge receipt of the complaint, assess whether interim measures are required, and then determine whether a formal investigation is warranted. Such investigations are generally assigned to a neutral individual who is external to the organization, such as a Chartered Industrial Relations Professional (CRIA) or a Chartered Human Resources Professional (CRHA).

## Immediate Legal Obligations

The Act Respecting Labour Standards requires every Québec employer to prevent psychological harassment and to put a stop to any situation brought to its attention. This is not an obligation to guarantee a particular outcome, but rather an obligation to take reasonable measures. The employer must be able to demonstrate that reasonable steps were taken in the circumstances.

Since the coming into force of the Act to Modernize the Occupational Health and Safety Regime (Bill 42), psychological harassment has been formally recognized as a specific type of psychosocial risk (PSR). Like physical hazards, it must be prevented, while still requiring a distinct legal analysis to determine whether the criteria set out in section 81.18 of the Act Respecting Labour Standards have been met. This recognition reinforces the employer’s obligation to act promptly, and a failure to do so may constitute a breach that engages the employer’s liability.

## Receiving the Complaint Without Prejudging It

The person receiving the complaint, whether a manager, an HR representative, or a member of senior leadership, is not required to determine immediately whether the complaint is well founded. Their role is limited to collecting the facts in a neutral manner, documenting what has been reported, and informing the complainant of the next steps.

Two common mistakes occur at this stage. The first is minimizing the complaint before the facts are known. The second is presuming the guilt of the respondent. Both can expose the employer to legal recourse, either for failing to meet its duty of prevention or for damaging the reputation of an individual who is later cleared of wrongdoing.

## Interim Measures to Consider

Pending the outcome of an investigation, the employer may need to take action to reduce risks or prevent the situation from escalating. Depending on the circumstances, this could include temporarily separating the parties involved, adjusting work schedules or work locations, or increasing oversight of communications between them.

These measures are not disciplinary in nature and should not be presented as such. Their purpose is to protect everyone involved, including the person who is the subject of the complaint, until the facts have been established.

## When and How to Launch a Formal Investigation

A formal investigation is appropriate whenever a complaint contains specific and verifiable allegations, such as identified incidents, dates, or potential witnesses, rather than a general sense of discomfort that may be better addressed through mediation. An investigation becomes essential when required by law, when the seriousness of the allegations warrants it, or when an initial attempt at informal resolution has failed.

The investigation mandate should be documented in writing and should clearly define the issues to be determined, the scope of the investigation, the expected level of confidentiality, and the anticipated timeline.

## Who Can Conduct the Investigation?

The law does not require that an investigation be conducted by an employer representative or by a lawyer. What is required is a neutral individual who is competent and independent from the situation. In the vast majority of cases, a CRIA or CRHA meets these criteria.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Is an employer required to investigate every harassment complaint?
No. However, every complaint must be assessed seriously.

How quickly must an employer act after receiving a complaint?
The law does not establish a single prescribed timeline, but it does require reasonable diligence.

Can an employee be dismissed before the investigation is completed?
This is rarely recommended.

Must a complaint be submitted in writing to be considered?
No. A verbal complaint must be taken just as seriously as a written complaint.
